Architecture62Chapter 1THE PROBLEM AND ITS SETTINGIntroductionAgriculture is a vital sector that sustains food production, supports livelihoods, and drives economic growth. However, the industry is undergoing a significant transformation driven by the integration of Information Technology, specifically the Internet of Things (IoT) and data analytics. This shift, often referred to as "Precision Agriculture," moves farming away from manual estimation toward data-driven decision-making to optimize resources. Within this framework, monitoring soil fertility is paramount, as elements such as nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, pH, temperature, and moisture play a crucial role in determining productivity (Hossain et al., 2024). To manage these complex variables, modern technologies combine IoT sensors for real-time data collection with machine learning algorithms that identify patterns and classify soil types according to their characteristics. This accurate classification enables the determination of suitable fertilizers and crops, promoting sustainable resource use. Reinforcing this technological approach, (Kethineni et al., 2024) demonstrated that such web-based platforms empower farmers to make informed decisions, significantly improving both yield and profitability.However, reliance on traditional soil management remains prevalent, given that conventional soil testing is costly and typically requires weeks to generate results, many farmers miss the optimal window for applying soil treatments (Louiella Samaniego & Gallego, 2024). Consequently, this delay forces farmers to resort to estimation; this “blind” application frequently leads to excessive chemical input, which accelerates soil acidification and contaminates nearby water sources (Vu Ngoc & Giang Trung, 2025). Furthermore, data availability alone is insufficient, as many smallholder farmers struggle to interpret technical nutrient reports, leaving them uncertain about the appropriate fertilizer strategy to implement (Goloran, 2025). In the Philippine context, (Kai et al., 2025) observed that organic farms in Sariaya, Quezon, and Los Baños, Laguna continue to experience low soil fertility due to limited nutrient availability and weak microbial activity, particularly when transitioning from chemical to organic farming. These challenges underscore the critical need for accessible, data-driven tools that empower farmers to understand their soil conditions and enhance agricultural productivity.	Therefore, this study proposes the Development of Soil Monitoring and Classification using IoT with Fertilizer Recommender System for Rice Farmers in Unisan, Quezon. The main goal is to modernize local rice farming by replacing manual guesswork with precise, data-driven technology. Specifically, the project aims to build an IoT device that collects real-time data on soil nutrients (Nitrogen, Phosphorus, Potassium), pH levels, and moisture. This data is then analyzed using Machine Learning to accurately determine the soil's health and identify what nutrients are missing. Finally, the system provides an easy-to-use web dashboard that automatically recommends the right fertilizer, helping rice farmers and the Municipal Agriculture Office (MAO) save money, improve crops, and protect the soil.	By improving soil understanding, preventing fertilizer misuse, and increasing crop productivity, this project contributes to sustainable agricultural practices aligned with the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), particularly Goal 2 (Zero Hunger) and Goal 12 (Responsible Consumption and Production). Moreover, it supports the University Research Agenda by promoting technological innovations that enhance community development and environmental conservation.Theoretical FrameworkThe theoretical foundation of this study is anchored on the Precision Agriculture Theory by (Robert, 2002), which focuses on using technology to collect and analyze agricultural data for better decision making and resource management. This theory highlights that accurate information about soil conditions, such as pH, NPK, and moisture levels, allows farmers to apply the right type and amount of fertilizer to improve yield and reduce waste. In the context of this research, the application of Precision Agriculture Theory is defined by two primary variables: Spatial Variability and Site-Specific Management (SSM). Spatial Variability refers to the quantitative differences in soil characteristics across different areas of the farm. In this system, this variable is operationalized through the specific soil parameters such as Nitrogen, Phosphorus, Potassium (NPK), pH, and moisture which are detected by the IoT sensors. Site Specific Management, on the other hand, represents the decision-making variable. It is the process of matching resource application to the specific needs of the soil. In this study, SSM is facilitated by the Fertilizer Recommender System, which translates the collected data into precise input instructions, ensuring that fertilizers are applied only where and when they are needed to optimize growth.Guided by this idea, the study developed a soil monitoring and classification system using IoT technology with a fertilizer recommender feature. The system aims to help farmers and the Municipal Agriculture Office (MAO) of Unisan, Quezon monitor soil health and make data-driven decisions that lead to sustainable farming practices. Using sensors and web-based analytics, the system provides real-time updates that support efficient fertilizer management and environmental protection, which are essential goals of modern precision agriculture.This theory supports the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) by (Davis, 1989), which explains how people decide to accept and use new technology. It focuses on two important factors, perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use.In this study, Perceived Usefulness is defined as the extent to which farmers and agricultural officers believe that the Soil Monitoring System will improve their operational efficiency and crop productivity. If the system effectively detects nutrient deficiencies and provides accurate recommendations, users will perceive it as a valuable tool. On the other hand, Perceived Ease of Use refers to how effortless the system is to operate. Given that some farmers may have limited technical skills, the hardware and web dashboard must be designed with a simple and intuitive interface to ensure that the technology is accessible and not burdensome to the user.The integration of these two theories creates a holistic framework that ensures both technical accuracy and user adoption. While the Precision Agriculture Theory guides the technical development of the system to ensure it captures precise data for site specific management, the Technology Acceptance Model ensures that this complex technology is designed in a way that is approachable for the end users. By balancing the scientific rigor of soil analysis with the practical requirements of user experience, the study guarantees that the proposed solution is not only functionally capable of improving soil health but also socially acceptable to the farming community in Unisan, Quezon.	In the context of this study, these factors determine how farmers and MAO staff will adopt and continue to use the soil monitoring and fertilizer recommender system. When the users find the system easy to operate and helpful in improving soil management, they are more likely to use it regularly. The combination of the Precision Agriculture Theory and TAM shows that the success of the proposed system depends not only on the accuracy of the technology but also on how users accept and utilize it to improve agricultural productivity.Conceptual FrameworkThe Development of Soil Monitoring and Classification using IoT with Fertilizer Recommender System is guided by a conceptual framework composed of the input, process, and output. Their study revealed that the primary barriers are the high financial cost and the lack of accessible technology. Farmers often view traditional laboratory testing as impractical due to the long turnaround time, which does not align with the immediate decision-making required during the planting season. Consequently, this forces them to rely on intuition rather than data, leading to suboptimal nutrient management.Similarly, Vu Ngoc & Giang Trung, 2025 statistically analyzed the consequences of this "blind" application. Their research demonstrated that estimation-based fertilization frequently leads to the over-application of nitrogen and phosphorus. This practice not only inflates production costs but also accelerates soil acidification and contributes to water pollution through nutrient runoff. The study argues that without precise monitoring tools, farmers unknowingly degrade the long-term fertility of their land while attempting to boost short-term yields.Further, Nakao et al., 2021 confirmed this long-term degradation in a longitudinal study of Philippine paddy soils. Comparing soil samples from the 1960s to the 2010s, they discovered a significant imbalance: while phosphorus levels had increased due to chemical fertilization, potassium levels had critically declined. This "mining effect" suggests that manual estimation fails to account for the complex nutrient uptake of rice crops, resulting in invisible but severe nutrient deficiencies that limit crop potential.Additionally, G. Vergara et al., 2024 identified that economic constraints further exacerbate these challenges, noting that financial instability often dictates fertilizer application rather than soil needs. Their study on agribusiness challenges found that farmers often delay or reduce fertilizer application based on their available cash flow or credit, rather than the crop's biological requirements. This disconnect between economic capacity and agronomic necessity is identified as a key operational failure of the traditional system.Moreover, Mishra et al., 2024 emphasized that traditional methods fail to monitor soil biological health. Their comparative analysis of farming scenarios showed that continuous chemical inputs without monitoring degrade soil microbial biomass, which is essential for nutrient cycling. They concluded that sustainable intensification is impossible without transitioning from manual estimation to data-driven soil health monitoring.Overall, the reviewed studies show that many farmers continue to rely on manual estimation and intuition due to the high cost and impracticality of traditional laboratory testing, which leads to nutrient imbalances, soil degradation, and economic inefficiency. While the negative impacts of "blind" fertilization are well-documented, there is still a gap, as most existing solutions are not tailored to the financial and operational realities of small-scale rice farmers in the Philippines. This underscores the urgent need for an affordable, localized IoT-based system that bridges the disconnect between agronomic requirements and farmers' economic.IoT-Based Soil Monitoring and Sensor Technologies	This section reviews studies on the technical components required for effective soil monitoring, focusing on sensor accuracy, data transmission protocols, and system integration strategies. It highlights the shift towards real-time data collection technologies that ensure reliability in agricultural environments.According to Adhikary et al., 2024, the use of NPK sensors based on Time Domain Reflectometry (TDR) is validated for real-time monitoring. Unlike traditional chemical analysis, TDR sensors measure the soil's dielectric constant to infer nutrient content instantly. Their study confirmed that these sensors are sufficiently accurate for precision agriculture and offer the durability needed for field deployment, making them a viable replacement for laboratory testing.In terms of data transmission, Hartono et al., 2024 demonstrated the superiority of the MQTT (Message Queuing Telemetry Transport) protocol over HTTP for soil monitoring systems. Their implementation showed that MQTT consumes significantly less power and bandwidth, which is critical for rural areas in the Philippines where cellular signals may be unstable. Their design successfully transmitted N, P, K, and pH data from remote fields to a cloud dashboard with minimal latency.Similarly, Sulaeman et al., 2024 developed and tested a portable soil nutrient detector specifically for irrigated and rainfed paddy soils. Their research highlighted the importance of integrating multiple parameters—N, P, K, pH, and electrical conductivity—into a single probe. Field tests in Java, Indonesia, proved that such portable IoT devices could provide readings comparable to standard laboratory results, offering a practical solution for on-site decision-making.The integration of these components into a robust system was further explored by Wacas, 2023 in the development of a smart greenhouse system. His study emphasized the need for edge computing capabilities within the microcontroller (e.g., Arduino or ESP32). By processing some data locally on the device, the system can trigger immediate actions (like alerts) even if internet connectivity is temporarily lost, ensuring continuous monitoring reliability.Finally, Dela Cruz et al., 2025 focused on the user accessibility of these hardware systems. In their "Digging Deeper" project, they designed a soil quality monitoring system that interfaces directly with Android smartphones. Their work confirms that for IoT hardware to be effective in rural communities, it must pair with familiar consumer electronics, allowing farmers to view sensor data on devices they already own.Overall, the reviewed literature establishes a clear technical consensus: robust soil monitoring requires TDR-based sensors for accuracy, MQTT protocols for efficient transmission in rural areas, and edge computing for reliability. Furthermore, the studies emphasize that hardware must be portable and integrated with familiar platforms like Android to be adoptable by farmers. However, while these individual components have been tested in isolation or in greenhouse settings, there is a lack of research integrating them into a cohesive, field-tested system specifically calibrated for the rice farming conditions of Unisan, Quezon.Machine Learning Algorithms for Fertilizer Recommendation	The third objective involves integrating a machine learning model to classify soil conditions and recommend fertilizers. Their results indicated that the Random Forest algorithm achieved the highest accuracy (84%) compared to Support Vector Machines (SVM) and Neural Networks. The study attributes this to Random Forest's ability to handle the high variance and noise typical of agricultural sensor data, making it the most robust choice for classifying soil fertility levels.Building on this, Akhilesh et al., 2025 utilized a Decision Tree Classifier to build a fertilizer recommendation system. Their study reported an accuracy of 99.78% in mapping soil parameters (N, P, K, pH) to specific fertilizer outputs. Their model outperformed traditional algorithms by using attention mechanisms to focus on the most critical soil features. This approach not only provides high classification accuracy but also offers feature importance scores, helping to explain why a specific nutrient is deficient.Latha & Kumaresan, 2025 further demonstrated the power of Deep Learning in their analytic perspective on soil nutrient prediction.
